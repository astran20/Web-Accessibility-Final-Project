THE TECH BEHIND THE DEMO

How virtual try-on works.

Augmented reality (AR) and artificial intelligence (AI) can show how a piece might look on you before you leave home. Here is how the real technology works, what our demo imitates, and why it matters for secondhand fashion.

THE PROBLEM

You can't tell how clothes fit until you try them on.

Fit is the top reason people return clothing, and many shoppers order several sizes just to send most of them back. For a secondhand shop, the same problem means wasted trips across town for pieces that don't work.

  • 45% of clothing returns were caused by poor fit in 2022. [1]
  • 61% of shoppers named poor fit as a main reason they return items. [2]
  • 1 truck of textiles is landfilled or burned every second, worldwide. [11]

TWO TECHNOLOGIES

AR shows it live. AI paints a picture.

AR try-on

Your live camera view, with clothes drawn on top as you move. Used by Snapchat's clothing lenses [5] and Amazon's shoe try-on [6].

  1. Camera. The app or web page opens your phone or laptop camera.
  2. Body tracking. A machine-learning model finds body landmarks (shoulders, elbows, hips, knees) in every frame. Google's free MediaPipe tool does this inside a web page. [7]
  3. Fitting. A 3D model of the garment is stretched and moved to match those points, and cloth simulation makes it hang and swing.
  4. Drawing. The garment is redrawn over the video many times a second.

Good: instant and interactive. Hard: needs a 3D model of every item, and can look like a sticker.

AI (generative) try-on

A still image of a person wearing the garment, created by an AI model. Used by Google Shopping's "try it on". [3] [4]

  1. Two photos. One of the person, one of the garment.
  2. Understanding the person. The system estimates their pose and removes the clothes they are wearing from the image.
  3. Generating. A diffusion model, the same kind of AI behind image generators, creates a new photo of the person wearing the garment, keeping patterns and wrinkles. [8]
  4. Training. Google's model learned from about 4 million image pairs and now works from a full-length photo or a selfie. [4]

Good: very realistic folds and shadows. Hard: slower, no live movement, and it shows how a piece looks, not whether it fits. [8]

RECOMMENDATIONS

How an AI stylist decides what to show you.

Recommendation systems suggest pieces you are likely to want. Modern shops pair them with a chat assistant, so you can describe what you want in your own words. [9]

Content-based filtering

Matches the features of each piece (type, size, color, price, style) to what you asked for or liked before.

You saved a brown leather jacket, so you see other warm outerwear.

Collaborative filtering

Recommends what shoppers with similar taste liked.

People who saved this jacket also saved these jeans.

OUR DEMO

What RETHREAD simulates, and what a real version would need.

This is a class prototype, so nothing on the site uses a camera, uploads a photo, or calls an AI service. Simple JavaScript imitates the experience, and every simulated feature is labeled.

What the RETHREAD demo does compared with a real version
Feature What our demo does What a real version would need
Your body Four illustrated sample models in sizes S to XL, with different builds and skin tones. Your camera or photo, with body tracking such as MediaPipe [7], and clear consent first.
The garment An illustrated version of each piece, drawn to the model's shape, with the real listing photo beside it. A 3D model of each piece (AR), or a hosted image model like Google's (AI). [3]
"Analyzing" steps A short animation showing the stages a real system goes through. Real body detection and garment mapping, taking a fraction of a second (AR) or several seconds (AI).

Style assistant Keyword matching: picks out type, size, budget, color and occasion, then ranks the 8 sample pieces. A language model to understand requests, plus recommendations learned from many shoppers.

DESIGNED WITH CARE

What a real try-on has to get right.

  • Privacy

    Face and body scans can count as biometric data. Retailers including Amazon have been sued in Illinois over try-on tools that scanned faces without written consent. [10] A real feature must explain what it captures, ask first, and offer a "use a model instead" option.

  • Honest results

    Try-on images are estimates. Shoppers should be told it is a preview, not a promise that something will fit.

  • Every body

    Models should cover many sizes, shapes and skin tones. Google's try-on models range from XXS to 4XL. [3]

  • Accessibility

    A camera-only feature leaves people out. Our demo works with a keyboard and screen reader, describes each result in text, and skips animations for people who turn them off.

  • One-of-a-kind pieces

    Every thrift item is unique, so shops can't build a 3D model of each one. Photo-based AI try-on suits secondhand clothing better than 3D AR.

SUSTAINABILITY

Fewer wasted trips. More clothes kept in use.

More than half of fast fashion is thrown away within a year. [11] Buying secondhand keeps clothes in use for longer, which is the main goal of a circular fashion economy. Try-on and recommendations help in three ways:

  • Fewer trips. Shoppers only travel for pieces that are likely to fit.
  • Fewer returns. Less shipping back and forth, and fewer items that never get resold.
  • The right buyer, sooner. One-of-a-kind pieces reach someone who will wear them, instead of sitting unsold.
